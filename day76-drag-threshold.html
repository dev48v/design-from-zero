A test suite has no hand: pointerDown, one pointerMove, pointerUp puts the pointer in two places with nothing in between, and the three things it can assert are a big move is a drag, no movement is a click, and the same big move in forty small steps is still a drag. TWO THINGS THAT DID NOT HAPPEN

The two lines nobody draws

Every draggable thing in every interface sits behind one decision that has no pixels: the press has happened, the pointer has moved a little, and something has to say whether this is a click on its way to being finished or a drag on its way to being started. Get it wrong in one direction and buttons stop working, because a hand that trembles by a pixel turns every click into a two-pixel drag of nothing. Get it wrong in the other and the thing you meant to move never lifts off.

The decision is worth exactly two lines of code, which is why it is almost never examined, and why so many different two-liners are in production. This page writes eight of them, hands all eight the same samples, and asks the question the two lines exist to answer.

1 · A test suite has no hand

Here is how a drag gets tested, in every codebase, in every framework: 

fireEvent.pointerDown(el, { clientX: 0,   clientY: 0 });
fireEvent.pointerMove(el, { clientX: 100, clientY: 0 });
fireEvent.pointerUp(el);
expect(onDragStart).toHaveBeenCalled();

Three events. The pointer is at the origin, then it is a hundred pixels away, and there is nothing in between — no intermediate samples, no overshoot, no tremor, and above all no time spent holding still. That gesture is not a poor approximation of a hand. It is a shape a hand cannot make.

A real press produces a sample every eight to sixteen milliseconds for as long as the button is down, and a resting hand is not still: it drifts a pixel here and a pixel there, forever, and the browser dutifully reports every one of them. A press held for a second is sixty to a hundred and twenty samples of noise before anything deliberate happens at all.

2 · The three things the test can assert

From the events above, a test can state three things without knowing anything about intent: a big move ends as a drag, no movement at all ends as a click, and the same big move delivered in forty small steps is still a drag. They are reasonable, they are the ones people write, and between them they look like they pin the behaviour down.

They do not. Six of the eight rules on this page satisfy all three, and five of those six are wrong. Not subtly wrong in a corner — the one that appears most often, any movement after the press is a drag, calls almost every deliberate click a drag, because the shake is movement and there is always some.

3 · What the eight rules actually differ by

All eight take the same list of samples and the same threshold. The threshold is not part of any of them — that matters, because otherwise a comparison of rules quietly becomes a comparison of constants. What is left is one clause each:

  • any movement — the pointer is not exactly where it went down.
  • distance travelled — add up the length of the path so far.
  • distance right now — how far it is from the press point at this sample.
  • either axis — the horizontal or the vertical distance, taken separately.
  • where it let go — compare the release point with the press point.
  • one fast jump — a single sample moved further than the threshold.
  • outside twice running — two samples in a row outside.
  • the furthest it got — it was ever outside, and once that is true it stays true.

The last one is the answer, and the reason is not that it is cleverer. It is that the intent — did this hand deliberately leave the neighbourhood of the press — is a statement about the maximum of a quantity over the gesture, and only one of the eight computes that maximum. Everything else measures something correlated with it.

4 · Why the path length is a clock in disguise

Adding up the distance travelled feels like the careful version: surely a gesture that has covered thirty pixels of ground is a drag. But a shaking hand covers ground without going anywhere. Each sample contributes roughly the mean length of one tremor step, so the total grows linearly with how long you have held the button down and not at all with how far you have moved.

That gives a prediction with no rule in it: the hold at which a still hand becomes a drag is the threshold divided by the mean step, which on the mouse model here is about seven samples, or fifty-six milliseconds. The page measures the longest hold still called a click and the shortest already called a drag, and checks the prediction lands between them. It does, on all three pointer types. A rule whose failure is a function of duration is a long-press detector that nobody meant to write.

5 · Why the release point loses the drop

Two of the rules ask only where the pointer ended up: distance right now, evaluated at the last sample, and where it let go. They are wrong on exactly one shape of gesture, and the page predicts that set before running them — the drags whose release landed back inside the threshold circle.

That shape is not exotic. It is picking a card up, changing your mind, and putting it back; dragging a slider out and returning it; a reorder that circles and lands where it started. Roughly a third of the deliberate drags in this corpus come home, and both rules report every one of them as a click — which in a real interface means the drop never fires and, worse, a click handler does.

The live version also has a second symptom the others do not: it un-latches. Because it asks the question fresh at every sample, a pointer that wanders back inside the circle mid-gesture makes it change its mind, and the page counts how often that happens. A rule that can flip back is a rule whose drag can be cancelled by a wobble.

6 · The corner the square cannot see

Comparing the horizontal and the vertical distance separately — |dx| > T || |dy| > T — is the most defensible of the wrong answers, and the hardest to catch, because it is right about almost everything. It is a square where the intent is a circle, and the square is larger: its area exceeds the inscribed circle's by exactly 4/π − 1, about 27.3%.

All of the excess is in the diagonals, which gives the exact shape of the failure. A steady move of length r in a uniformly random direction is missed with probability 1 − (4/π)·acos(T/r) for r between T and T√2, and never outside that ring. The page sweeps three thousand six hundred directions and matches the law to within what a grid that fine can resolve. On real gestures the ring is narrow but not empty: the short diagonal nudge — moving something by a hair — falls in it, and the square misses all of them.

7 · The control

It would be easy to conclude that the shake is what breaks these rules, and easy to be wrong about it, so the page runs a paired control: the same generator, the same seed, the same sample counts, the same deliberate excursions, with the shake amplitude set to zero. That corpus is precisely what a test harness produces.

Taking the shake out removes every single false drag — all of them, on every rule — and about three per cent of the missed drags. So the shake is the whole story for one failure mode and essentially none of the story for the other. The square still misses the diagonal nudge with a perfectly steady hand; the release-point rules still lose the drop. Those are errors of the clause, not of the noise, and no amount of smoothing will touch them.

8 · The rule the properties throw out

The most uncomfortable result on this page is the inversion at the other end. Outside twice running — require two consecutive samples outside the circle before committing — is the second most accurate of the eight, right on 99.7% of the corpus, and it fails the very first blind property.

It fails because the test's gesture has exactly one sample outside the circle: the teleport. There is no second one, because there is nothing between the two positions. The page predicts this exactly: the dwell rule differs from the correct one on precisely the gestures that cross the circle for a single sample and no more, of which there are eight in the whole hand-driven corpus and every single one of the teleports. A test suite is not merely blind to five wrong rules — it actively rejects a good one, for a shape only it can produce.

9 · The threshold, which is a separate question

Because the threshold was handed to all eight rules, it can be studied on its own. Sweeping it from zero upward and asking where the correct rule makes no mistake at all gives a band per pointer type, and the bands are pushed from both ends: from below by the shake, which sets how far a still hand can wander, and from above by the shortest move anybody deliberately makes.

The intended claim going in was that no single threshold can serve a mouse and a touchscreen at once. Measured, that is false: the three bands do intersect, and a single value sits inside all of them. What is true is much less dramatic and more useful — the intersection is only a few pixels wide, and it is narrow for a reason you can name from the two ends that squeeze it. That is the sort of claim a measurement is for.

10 · What to take away

The properties everyone asserts about a drag threshold are all true of five implementations that are wrong, and false of one that is nearly right. This is not because the properties are badly written. It is because they are evaluated on gestures a test constructs, and the entire difficulty of the problem lives in the samples a test does not construct: the ones in between, and the ones that happen while nothing is happening.

If you take one thing: the quantity to threshold is the maximum distance from the press point over the whole gesture, latched, and it should be measured as a distance and not per axis. The other seven each measure something that agrees with it on the gestures you will write a test for.

Build it

Nine steps. Everything between ENGINE:BEGIN and ENGINE:END in this file is pure functions over arrays of samples — no DOM, no clock, no Math.random — which is what makes it possible to run the same code over a generated corpus in a self-check and over the reader's own hand on the pad above.

1 · Decide what a gesture is

A gesture is a list of {x, y, t} from pointerdown to pointerup. The first entry is the press and it carries no shake, because pointerdown's coordinates are the origin by definition. Everything the rules do is a function of this list and one number. 

el.addEventListener('pointerdown', e => {
  el.setPointerCapture(e.pointerId);
  samples = [{ x: e.clientX, y: e.clientY, t: e.timeStamp }];
});
el.addEventListener('pointermove', e => {
  if (!samples) return;
  samples.push({ x: e.clientX, y: e.clientY, t: e.timeStamp });
  decide(samples);
});

Use setPointerCapture. Without it the pointer leaving the element ends the gesture, and a drag that leaves the element is the entire point of a drag.

2 · Write the rule as a maximum, and latch it

function isDrag(samples, T) {
  for (let i = 1; i < samples.length; i++) {
    const dx = samples[i].x - samples[0].x;
    const dy = samples[i].y - samples[0].y;
    if (dx * dx + dy * dy >= T * T) return i;   // latched: never re-tested
  }
  return -1;
}

Two details do all the work. It compares squared distances, so there is no square root and no floating-point boundary to argue about. And it returns the index, not a boolean: the sample at which the drag started is where the UI must pick the item up from, and a rule that cannot say when it committed cannot place the thing it is dragging.

3 · Never re-ask the question

In real code the latch is a flag on the gesture state, set once: 

if (!state.dragging) {
  const at = isDrag(samples, T);
  if (at >= 0) { state.dragging = true; state.origin = samples[at]; onDragStart(samples[at]); }
}

The version without if (!state.dragging) is the distance right now rule, and it is the single most common way to accidentally write a drag that cancels itself when the user brings the pointer back near where they started.

4 · Do not add up the path

Accumulating hypot(dx, dy) between consecutive samples looks more thorough and is a duration detector: a still hand adds a pixel or two per sample forever. If you want a long-press, write a long-press; do not get one by accident.

5 · Do not compare the axes separately

Math.abs(dx) > T || Math.abs(dy) > T is a square. It needs √2 times as much movement on the diagonal, and the whole of the discrepancy is concentrated exactly where short deliberate nudges live.

6 · Give touch its own number

A finger's contact patch wanders several pixels while it is down and a mouse's does not, so one threshold applied to both either eats short mouse drags or turns taps into drags. Read event.pointerType and keep a small table. Everything else about the rule stays identical — the threshold is data, the clause is the code.

7 · Generate the corpus from intents, not from verdicts

To test any of this you need gestures whose right answer is known independently. Build them from an intent — press and hold, flick, slow haul, out and back, diagonal nudge — and then shake them. Because the intent came first, no rule can be scored against a label that a rule produced.

Then throw away the ambiguous ones. A gesture generated as a drag whose furthest excursion did not clear its own threshold has no defensible right answer, so it is discarded and the discards are counted. Two of 2,722 here.

8 · Keep a control arm

Run the same generator with the shake set to zero. Everything else identical. Any difference between the arms is attributable to the shake and nothing else — which is how the page can say the shake is responsible for all of one failure mode and almost none of the other, rather than assuming it.

9 · Assert bounds and identities, never constants

The self-check on this page never compares anything to a number typed in by hand. Every check is either a bound the arithmetic cannot leave — a latch index is a real sample index, a rule cannot be right more often than it ran — or two independent computations of the same quantity being compared: a closed form against a measurement, the correct rule against the definition of the intent, the shaken arm against the steady one. A test that hardcodes today's number passes forever and means nothing.
