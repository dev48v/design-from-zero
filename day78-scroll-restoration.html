DESIGNFROMZERO · DAY 78

⏫ Scroll restoration — you scroll a feed, tap an item, press Back, and the list is supposed to put you where you were. THE FEED IS SIMULATED, NOT A BROWSER: items with real heights, a viewport, a load history and six restore policies, over 600 enumerated journeys per policy, so every figure is a count and a 0 is a zero. 🔴 The policy in the most code — save scrollTop, set it back — is exact on 600 of 600 journeys when nothing changes, and gets 0 of 600 right the moment three items arrive at the top of the feed. It is not slightly off: the reader lands a mean of 2.86 items from where they were and up to 9 at the worst. 🔴 And it passes every property a scroll-restoration test asserts, because those properties are about the number and not about the reader. 🔴 Meanwhile the policy that FAILS the suite puts the reader on the right item in 600 of 600 — remembering which item was on top is rejected because it restores to 0 when the reader was part-way down the first post, while being exactly right about the thing that matters.

Computed in your browser on load. The engine does not drive a browser — it models the list: item heights, a viewport, how many pages had loaded, and what arrived at the top while the reader was away. A separate verifier lifts the engine out of this file and re-derives every figure under Node.

WHAT THE READER SEES

“Exact” means the same item is on top and the reader is within two pixels of the same place inside it. “Items off” is what they would actually notice — a forty-pixel slip is invisible, one whole item is not.

1. The properties are true, and they are about the number

A scroll-restoration test can assert three things without a reader in the room: that a position gets set, that it lands inside the document, and that scrolling further before leaving restores further. Every one is a real statement about the code.

Four policies satisfy all of it. Only two of the four are correct once the feed moves — and the suite has no way to tell which, because none of the three properties mentions the item the reader was looking at.

2. A static fixture cannot see the bug

Here is the same policy measured twice: once on a feed that did not change while the reader was away, and once on a feed that gained three items at the top. That is not an edge case — it is what a feed does.

Save-scrollTop goes from 600 of 600 to 0 of 600. A pixel offset is a promise about a coordinate system, and inserting anything above the reader silently redefines it. The test fixture is static because writing a fixture that grows is more work, and that single convenience is what hides the defect.

3. The suite rejects the policy that works

Remembering which item was on top — and nothing else — fails the first property, it sets a scroll position at all. When the reader was part-way down the very first post, this policy restores to 0, and a suite that treats “restored nothing” as a bug records a failure. It is a genuine failure of a genuine property, on 5 of 400 checks.

And it is right about the item on 600 of 600 journeys, mean distance 0.000 items. The reader is standing exactly where they were, give or take how far down one post they had scrolled. The suite throws it out and keeps the policy that is a mean of 2.86 items wrong.

What this fixture cannot tell you

Two of the six policies — index-offset and id-offset — score identically here, 600 of 600 on every setting. They are not the same policy: one finds the item by position, the other by identity, and they come apart when an item is removed rather than added. This fixture never removes anything, so it cannot distinguish them, and reporting them as one row would hide that. They are listed separately and scored separately for exactly that reason.

How it works

A feed is a list of heights — text posts around 90–160px, image posts 280–420, the occasional 520–700 embed — because a ragged list is the whole reason index-based and pixel-based restoration disagree. A journey records where the reader was as (item, offset into it), which is the ground truth, and then each policy proposes a scroll position to come back to. 

// the one in the most code
restore: (j) => j.y            // a NUMBER

// what the reader actually meant
restore: (j) => scrollFor(j.items, j.anchor.index + j.prepend, j.anchor.into)

The draws come from a seeded Park-Miller generator rather than Math.random, so every journey is reproducible and a verifier can re-derive the same 600. The draw below n takes the high bits: (nextInt() * n) >>> 31 would be wrong in JavaScript, because bitwise operands are coerced to 32 bits and the product overflows that for any n above 1.
