▼ Scroll-spy — the table of contents that lights the heading you are reading. One integer to settle, and the thing everybody tests about it is exactly one entry is lit, it never jumps backwards, and it is on screen. That is satisfied at every one of 21,909 whole pixels of scroll across three real documents by seven of these nine implementations — and six of those seven are wrong, because a heading that is in the table of contents never lights at all: 8 of 34 entries, named below. The textbook reading line is not buggy — it satisfies the reading line is inside the lit section at 100% of pixels, and that property is provably incompatible with every entry lights on any page whose last headings sit inside the final screen. So you must break one of them, and the correct rule breaks the first one by the least it can: 8 clamped thresholds, all 8 forced, worst early activation 608px against a ceiling of 704. Also here: never moving backwards is vacuous — 0 backward steps in 11,557,377 comparisons, and a two-line proof of why; click an entry and it lights is capped by a pigeonhole the correct rule hits exactly; and on the standard six-equal-sections fixture seven of nine pass every test, including a rule that ignores section heights entirely. 58,019,556 assertions on load. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

    🧠 What is actually going on

    A scroll-spy is the smallest interesting component on a documentation site: a list of headings, and one of them lit. There is exactly one number to compute — the index of the lit entry — and the obvious way to compute it is right about the middle of the page and silently wrong about the end of it.

    1. The rule everybody writes, and what it actually promises

    Put a reading line a fixed distance below the top of the window, at s + off where s is the scroll position. Light the last heading whose top has passed it. It is three lines, it is monotone, the lit section is always on screen, and the reading line is always inside it. Every property you would think to assert holds at every single pixel.

    What it promises, precisely, is: the lit entry is the section the reading line is in. That promise is kept perfectly. The problem is that the line does not go everywhere.

    2. The line stops before the document does

    The scroll ends at M = D − view, so the reading line sweeps from off to M + off — which is D − (view − off), not D. Everything in the last view − off pixels of the document is below the line forever. If a heading starts in there, the line never reaches it, and the rule that promises “the section the line is in” can never name it.

    This is not a rare shape. It is what the end of a documentation page looks like: Browser compatibility, See also, License. Three short sections in a row and three entries in the table of contents that no amount of scrolling will ever light.

    3. So the two properties collide, and one of them has to go

    Write them down together:

    • Inside: whenever entry i is lit, the reading line is inside section i.
    • Reachable: for every entry i there is some scroll position where i is lit.

    If top_i > M + off then no scroll position puts the line inside section i, so Inside forbids ever lighting it and Reachable demands it. The two cannot both hold. Not “usually conflict” — cannot. And the choice is not symmetric: violating Inside means an entry lights slightly early, which nobody notices; violating Reachable means an entry in your navigation is dead, which is the bug report.

    4. Which turns the whole thing into a table, not a test

    Once you accept that some entries must light early, you cannot answer the question from the current scroll position alone — how early entry i has to be depends on how many entries come after it. So compute the whole table once. Entry i takes over at scroll t_i, and: 

    t_i = clamp(top_i - off,  i,  max(i, M - (n-1-i)))
      natural value    lower    upper

    The lower bound says every earlier entry already used a pixel. The upper bound says every later entry still needs one. Clamping a strictly increasing sequence between two strictly increasing bounds leaves it strictly increasing — that single sentence is the whole correctness proof, and it gives you Reachable for free, because strictly increasing thresholds means every entry owns a non-empty range of scroll.

    5. And the clamp is the least you can pay

    The upper bound is not a taste decision. If entry i took over even one pixel later there would be fewer than n−1−i pixels left, and one of the entries after it would have nowhere to live. So among all rules that are monotone and light every entry, the clamped value is the latest possible, and therefore the early activation it pays is the smallest any correct rule can pay. On these three documents that is 1,047, 909 and 665 pixels spread across the clamped entries, worst case 608px — and it can never exceed view − off, which is 704.

    6. The test everybody writes cannot pass, and that is arithmetic too

    Click the entry, assert the entry lights. Clicking entry i scrolls to top_i − off, clamped into [0, M]. Every entry starting inside the final screen clamps to the same value: M. So the rule is handed one input and asked for k different outputs. At least k − 1 of them are wrong for any rule at all. The threshold table is wrong on exactly k − 1 — it hits the bound — and a test asserting 100% is asserting something no implementation can deliver.

    The same test is savage about a defect nobody would call important: change ≤ to < and the click round trip collapses from 26 of 34 to 3, because the click lands the line exactly on the heading top, which is the one pixel where the two rules disagree.

    7. Nothing flickers, and the reason is worth knowing

    The other thing everybody asserts is that the highlight does not jump around. It cannot. As s grows by one pixel, at most one section gains a visible pixel (the one the bottom edge is inside) and at most one loses one (the one the top edge is inside). Every other section is unchanged. So each section's visible height rises then falls, they rise and fall in index order, and the argument-maximum of an ordered family of unimodal functions moves forward only. That covers “most visible”, “largest fraction”, “nearest”, every threshold rule and the scroll-percentage rule — 0 backward steps in 11,557,377 adjacent-pixel comparisons.

    The flicker people write hysteresis to fix is real, but it comes from state, not from the rule. The canonical IntersectionObserver band recipe keeps the previous answer whenever the callback reports nothing — and it reports nothing for 87.6% of the scroll on a long page. That makes it a function of the path, not the position: reach the same pixel going up rather than down and the answer is different on 87.6% of the page, always by exactly one entry. A test that scrolls in one direction cannot see any of it.

    8. What the fixture cannot see, and why that is computable

    Six identical 900px sections. M = 4,600, and the last heading's top is at 4,500, which reaches the line at 4,404 — comfortably inside the scroll. So on that fixture every entry lights costs nothing, the click round trip costs nothing, and seven of the nine rules pass every test in the file, including the one that maps scroll percentage straight onto the entry count and never looks at a section height at all. The fixture separates exactly two rules, and both of them by the same one-pixel boundary.

    Generating more fixtures does not help: 80 random documents built the same way separate nothing. Change one thing — let a quarter of the sections be short, the way a real page has a short “See also” — and the reading line loses an entry on 56.3% of pages. Make every section short, the way a changelog is, and it is 100.0%. Same code, same oracle, same assertions. The corpus was the test.

    🔨 How it is built

    1. One block of pure functions, and no DOM in it

    Everything between ENGINE:BEGIN and ENGINE:END is arithmetic over pixel offsets. No document, no window, no clock, no Math.random. The verifier lifts that block straight out of the file with a regular expression, evaluates it, and refuses to run if it finds a DOM access — which means the thing being checked is exactly the thing the page runs, not a copy of it.

    2. The document model is three arrays

    Section heights, prefix sums for the tops, and the document height. Sections tile the document with no gaps, so section i owns [top_i, top_i + h_i) and the last one ends at D. That is the only geometry on the page; everything else is comparisons against s + off.

    3. Nine rules, one clause apart

    Each implementation is a single active(P, view, off, s, pre) that returns an index. They are deliberately written as neighbours — ≤ versus <, most pixels versus largest fraction, with and without the force-the-last-one patch — so that a difference in the tables is a difference in one clause and not in one program.

    4. The threshold table is built with two clamps and no search

    The lower bound is i; the upper is max(i, M − (n−1−i)). The max is not decoration: on a page too short to give every entry a pixel the upper bound would fall below the lower one, and letting the lower one win keeps the table strictly increasing so the reader is honest about which entries are unreachable rather than silently returning the wrong one.

    5. Every number is computed twice, from different shapes

    The lit entry comes out of a binary search over the threshold table. The same answer is rebuilt as explicit activation intervals [t_i, t_{i+1} − 1], checked to tile [0, M] with no gap, no overlap and no empty range, and then read linearly at every pixel. Both readings agree at 21,909 of 21,909 scroll positions, every load.

    6. A whole-pixel sweep, not a sample

    Nothing here is sampled. The three real documents are swept at every integer scroll position — 8,097, 11,471 and 2,341 of them — for all nine rules and six properties, and the corpora add 1,255,497 more scroll positions. Sampling would have missed the one-character defect entirely: it lives on 23 pixels out of 21,909.

    7. The stateful rule is swept twice, on purpose

    The band rule is not a function of s, so a single sweep does not describe it. It is run downward from 0 to M and upward from M to 0, both traces kept, and the disagreement counted per pixel. That is the only way the path dependence shows up as a number instead of a hunch.

    8. What the verifier does differently

    None of the oracles shares a representation with the page. The document set is restated as literals and checked control by control. The lit entry is recomputed by a linear scan with no binary search, and again from a fully expanded scroll-position → entry array built once per document. The threshold table is re-derived by constraint propagation — repeatedly tightening bounds until they stop moving — rather than by clamping in one pass. Reachability is recomputed as a set of distinct values in the expanded array. The click bound is re-derived by counting collisions among clamped targets. And the monotonicity claim is checked by an independent unimodality argument over per-section visible heights. Every percentage the page prints is recomputed, not restated.
