DESIGNFROMZERO · DAY 83

Presence cursors — a peer's position arrives about fifteen times a second, so 95% of what you see between those packets is something you made up on purpose.

Step 1 — what you are actually building is a clock

A multiplayer cursor looks like a drawing problem and is really a timing problem. Every other person in the file is a stream of sparse position samples that arrive late, out of order, and sometimes not at all, and your job is to turn that stream into something that moves like a hand. Almost nothing in the visible result is data you received — the data is maybe fifteen positions a second, and the screen redraws sixty times a second. So most frames are yours to invent, and the whole craft of this feature is in how you invent them. Hold that idea and the rest of the page is just consequences of it.

One deterministic replay of the demo, measured: how many frames the renderer drew, and how many of them were backed by a packet that had actually arrived on that frame.

Step 2 — why you interpolate instead of teleporting

The lazy version assigns the newest position straight to the cursor element and lets the browser paint it. It works, and it looks broken: the cursor sits perfectly still for a tenth of a second and then jumps sixty pixels, which reads as lag even when the network is fine. The eye is extremely good at spotting a thing that moves in discrete hops, because nothing physical does. Interpolation replaces the hop with a straight line walked over the time between two samples, so the cursor is always moving and never more than a frame's worth of distance from where it should be. The cost is that you are always showing a position the peer has already left — which is fine, because being smoothly a little behind reads as presence, and being exactly right in lurches reads as a bug.

Same replay, same packets. The only difference is whether the frames between two packets are drawn or skipped.

Step 3 — why the renderer has to run in the past

To interpolate between two samples you need both of them, and the second one has not arrived yet. The fix is to render on a delayed clock: draw what the world looked like one network trip ago, so the sample you need to interpolate towards is already sitting in the buffer. Pick the delay too small and you run off the end of the buffer on every frame and fall back to holding the last known point, which is exactly the jumping you were trying to remove. Pick it too large and the cursors visibly trail the conversation. One latency, plus the jitter, plus one update interval of headroom is the usual answer, and it is the single number that most changes how this feature feels.

Each row is the whole replay re-run with a different render delay. held means the renderer had nothing in front of it and froze on the last known point; it is the row that teleports.

Step 4 — why you throttle what you send

A pointer generates an event per frame and sometimes more, so an unthrottled cursor channel is sixty-plus messages a second per person. Ten people in a file is six hundred messages a second through a socket that also has to carry the actual edits, and the receiving end cannot draw any of it faster than its own refresh anyway. Throttling to somewhere around fifty milliseconds cuts that by three quarters and changes nothing anyone can see, because the interpolator fills the gap. The important detail is that throttling here is not sampling: you keep the newest position in a single slot and overwrite it, so what you finally send is where the pointer is, not where it happened to be at a tick boundary.

A fixed 16 ms clock over one second, so the counts are exact rather than whatever the machine felt like doing that run.

Step 5 — why the trailing edge is the half everyone forgets

A leading-edge throttle fires immediately and then ignores everything until the interval is up. If the pointer stops moving inside that window — which is what happens every time someone finishes a gesture and parks the cursor on a word — the final position is sitting in the pending slot when the events stop, and nothing ever sends it. The peer's cursor ends up frozen short of where it really is, by up to one interval's worth of travel, and it stays wrong until that person moves again. The trailing edge is the rule that says: if there is a pending sample and the interval has elapsed, send it even though no new event arrived. It costs one timer, and it is the difference between cursors that land where people put them and cursors that are permanently a little bit lost.

The pointer moves to x=220 and stops dead. Leading-edge only never reports it, at all, ever.

Step 6 — why the receiver must distrust the order

Latency is not a constant, it is a distribution, so two messages sent 64 ms apart can arrive in the wrong order. If you append every arriving sample to the buffer in arrival order, one reordered packet puts a point from the past at the head of your timeline, and the interpolator dutifully walks the cursor backwards and then forwards again — a visible stutter caused by a packet that was otherwise harmless. The fix is that every message carries the sender's timestamp and the buffer rejects anything not strictly newer than its last entry. Dropped packets then need no handling at all beyond this: a gap in the timeline is simply a longer segment to interpolate across, which is why a lossy cursor channel still looks smooth.

Seeded transport with a fixed latency floor, uniform jitter and a drop rate. Nothing here calls the global random source, so these numbers are the same on every machine.

Step 7 — why presence needs a timeout, not a leave event

The obvious design is that a client sends join when it opens the file and leave when it closes, and the server broadcasts both. It fails in the single most common case: nobody sends leave when the laptop lid shuts, the tunnel drops, the tab is killed, or the process is force-quit. The ghost cursor that lingers for an hour after someone left the meeting is always this bug. Presence has to be derived from evidence rather than announced — a peer exists while packets keep arriving, and is removed when none has arrived for a few seconds. A leave message is then only an optimisation that makes the disappearance prompt; the timeout is what makes it correct, and this engine has no leave message at all.

One peer stops transmitting at t=1200 ms with no goodbye of any kind, and is gone one timeout later.

Step 8 — why the colour must come from the id

The tempting implementation is a palette and a counter: the first person to connect is amber, the second is rose, and so on. It survives exactly until someone's connection blips. They drop, the timeout removes them, they reconnect two seconds later, and now they are a different colour — and so is everybody who joined after them, if you ever compact the list. Colour is identity here, and identity cannot depend on arrival order or on how many other people happen to be in the room. Take the hue straight off a hash of the stable user id — a continuous wheel rather than eight swatches, because five people in eight slots collide four times out of five — and the same person is the same colour in every session, on every client, forever, with no coordination between clients at all.

The same five peers, with what a join-order palette would have given each of them had one person reconnected first.

Step 9 — why the label flips instead of being clipped

The name tag hangs down and to the right of the cursor hotspot, which is correct for about ninety percent of the canvas and wrong at every edge. Near the right border the tag runs off the viewport and the name is cut in half; near the bottom it disappears under the fold. Clipping is the worst option, because a half-visible name is unreadable and still takes the space. The rule is the one a tooltip uses: compute the preferred box, test it against the viewport, flip it to the opposite side of the anchor if it does not fit, and clamp only as a last resort when the tag is wider than the space on either side. The flip has to be per-axis — a cursor in the bottom-right corner flips both, one at the top-right flips only horizontally.

A 92×18 tag in a 640×320 viewport, at nine anchor positions.

Step 10 — why the whole thing has to be replayable

Every component above is time-dependent and most of them are probabilistic, which makes the naive version of this feature untestable: it works on your machine, it is janky on someone else's, and you have no way to tell whether you fixed anything. Pushing the randomness into a seeded generator and the clock into an explicit fixed step turns the entire system into a pure function from (seed, settings) to a list of frames. Now a regression is a changed digest, a tuning decision is a table of numbers instead of an argument, and the self-check at the end of this page can assert real behaviour rather than that nothing threw. It is also why the demo is driven by a counter rather than by a wall clock — the same replay on a 30 Hz screen produces exactly the same positions.

Four seeds, same settings. The digest is an FNV-1a hash over every rendered position of every frame.

The interpolator

Pure, and the only function that decides what a cursor is doing between two packets. It clamps at both ends rather than extrapolating: guessing past the newest known point assumes a velocity the peer may already have abandoned, so every stop overshoots, and the correction afterwards is more visible than the delay it saved.
